Dandelion (Taraxacum officinale)

What is dandelion traditionally used for?

Traditional use of dandelion dates back centuries in Arabian, Chinese, Ayurvedic, and European systems, where it was used for digestive, liver, and diuretic support.

What does published research say about dandelion?

A 2021 comprehensive review in the Bulletin of the National Research Centre examined 54 studies and reported research on diuretic, hepatoprotective, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and other activities. A 2022 review focused on gastrointestinal potential, noting compounds such as taraxasterol, phenolic acids, and polysaccharides studied in relation to digestive and liver-related models. More recent 2023–2025 reviews continue to summarize phytochemicals (flavonoids, phenolic acids, terpenes) and preclinical findings on antioxidant and related effects.

Clinical evidence in humans remains limited for many traditional uses; most data are preclinical or small-scale.

Milk Thistle (Silybum marianum)

What is milk thistle good for?

Milk thistle's primary active complex, silymarin, has been extensively studied for liver-related endpoints. A 2020 narrative review summarized clinical observations in alcoholic and non-alcoholic fatty liver contexts, noting antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms.

Multiple meta-analyses — including 2024–2025 analyses of randomized trials in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (NAFLD) — have reported reductions in liver enzymes (ALT, AST) and improvements in certain metabolic markers in some populations. Results vary by dose, duration, and study quality, and histological benefits are not consistently demonstrated.

Is milk thistle an established treatment for liver disease?

No. Authoritative summaries, including those referencing Cochrane and major guidelines, note that evidence is mixed and of variable quality — silymarin is not established as a primary treatment for any liver disease.

Mullein (Verbascum thapsus)

What is mullein traditionally used for?

Mullein has a long traditional history in European and other systems as a demulcent and expectorant for respiratory comfort, particularly dry or irritative coughs. Reviews note mucilage (soothing) and saponins (traditionally linked to expectorant action), along with anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial activity in laboratory models.

What do regulators say about mullein?

The European Medicines Agency recognizes certain mullein preparations as traditional herbal medicinal products for relief of sore throat associated with dry cough and cold — based on long-standing use rather than modern clinical trials.

High-quality human clinical trials specifically testing mullein leaf or tea for cough or lung function are lacking, and claims of "detoxing" or "cleansing" the lungs are not supported by clinical evidence.

Burdock (Arctium lappa)

What is burdock root traditionally used for?

Burdock root has traditional use in European and Asian systems as a depurative (a traditional "cleansing" herb) and for skin and digestive support.

What does modern research say about burdock?

Modern reviews describe antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and other activities in preclinical models. Small clinical studies have examined effects on inflammatory markers (for example in knee osteoarthritis) and certain metabolic parameters, and a 2024 review summarized pharmacological properties including hepatoprotective and metabolic findings in experimental settings. Clinical evidence remains limited.

Plantain (Plantago major)

What is plantain leaf used for? (Is this the banana-like fruit?)

No — this is Plantago major, the common broadleaf (and narrow-leaf) plantain herb, not the starchy banana-like fruit. Its leaves have been used traditionally worldwide for wound support and respiratory and digestive comfort.

A classic 2000 review documented wound-healing, anti-inflammatory, and related activities linked to polysaccharides, flavonoids, and iridoid glycosides. More recent reviews (including 2025) continue to explore anti-inflammatory and potential respiratory effects in experimental models; limited clinical data exist, for example in combination products for cough.

Purslane (Portulaca oleracea)

Why is purslane considered nutritious?

Purslane is recognized as one of the richer terrestrial plant sources of omega-3 fatty acids (particularly alpha-linolenic acid), along with antioxidants such as vitamin E, vitamin C, and glutathione.

A foundational 1992 study in the Journal of the American College of Nutrition quantified these nutrients and compared them favorably to spinach. Comprehensive 2024 reviews confirm the nutritional profile and summarize preclinical findings on antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and related properties.

Yellow Dock (Rumex crispus)

What is yellow dock root traditionally used for?

Yellow dock root has traditional use as a mild laxative, astringent, and "blood cleanser" / alterative in European and other herbal systems. A 2024 comprehensive review catalogued its phytochemicals (anthraquinones, flavonoids, and others) and summarized traditional uses alongside preclinical pharmacological data.

Are there cautions with yellow dock?

Yes. Caution is advised due to oxalate and anthraquinone content — long-term or high-dose use is not recommended without professional guidance. Clinical trial evidence for specific therapeutic claims is sparse.

Oregano (Origanum vulgare)

What are the active compounds in oregano?

Oregano is rich in phenolic compounds and essential-oil constituents — notably carvacrol and thymol — with well-documented antimicrobial and antioxidant activity in laboratory studies.

Reviews confirm broad-spectrum activity against various bacteria and fungi in vitro and describe mechanisms such as membrane disruption. Limited clinical data explore topical or other applications.

Wormwood (Artemisia absinthium)

What is wormwood traditionally used for?

Wormwood has a long history as a traditional bitter and anthelmintic. The European Medicines Agency recognizes certain preparations for temporary loss of appetite and mild digestive complaints, based on traditional use.

Is wormwood safe?

Wormwood contains thujone, which can be neurotoxic in excess. Products intended for internal use are typically regulated for low thujone content, and essential oil should not be ingested. Safety data support short-term, appropriately prepared use in adults — it is not recommended in pregnancy.
